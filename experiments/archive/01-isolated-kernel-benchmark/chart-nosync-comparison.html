Isolated kernel speedup, default vs. tuned — does removing per-iteration sync change the answer?

Both panels use the identical fully-randomized, cross-shape, cache-cycling design (Run 3 there): 700,000 launches, 5 shapes × 35 M-points × 2 configs × 2000 iterations, uniformly random weight-copy per launch. Every isolated benchmark in this project — including that one — calls torch.accelerator.synchronize() before every single kernel launch, forcing the GPU to fully drain between calls (inherited verbatim from vLLM's own tuner script). Real serving never does this: a decode step replays as a CUDA graph, dozens of kernels back-to-back on one stream with zero host sync in between. The bottom panel removes that per-iteration sync entirely — kernels queue back-to-back, timed via CUDA events, with a single synchronize() only after all 700,000 launches are queued — to test whether that difference was hiding the real-serving regression.

With per-iteration sync (original methodology)
Overall mean +6.5%, median +6.6%. gate_up_proj M=2048 (the point that shows the real-serving regression): +0.7% — flat.
Without per-iteration sync — kernels queued back-to-back
A real effect, but not the one we were looking for. Overall mean nearly doubles to +12.8% (median +12.3%) — broadly, across almost every point, not one outlier — so removing artificial per-iteration idle gaps genuinely does change tuned's average isolated advantage. But at gate_up_proj M=2048 specifically, the point that actually matters, it moves the wrong way for the hypothesis: +0.7% → +1.2%, still flat, no hint of the real-serving regression. The negative anchors nearby (M=512: −9.6%→−12.8%, M=1024: −3.5%→−5.6%) got modestly more negative, while M=2048 itself stayed pinned near zero either way. Per-iteration sync removal is a real, separate effect on the overall level — it isn't what's hiding the M=2048-specific regression.
